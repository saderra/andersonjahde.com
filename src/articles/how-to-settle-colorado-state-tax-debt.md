If you owe income tax to the Colorado Department of Revenue and cannot pay it in full, you have fewer options than you would with the IRS, and the Department moves faster than most taxpayers expect. This article explains the realistic ways to resolve a Colorado state tax debt, how they differ from the federal programs you may have read about, and the missteps that turn a manageable balance into liens, levies, and garnished wages.

## First, understand what the Department can do

The Colorado Department of Revenue (the "Department") is a creditor with statutory collection powers that ordinary creditors do not have. Once a tax has been assessed and a notice and demand for payment has gone unanswered, the Department can, among other things:

- Record a tax lien against your real and personal property.
- Levy your bank accounts.
- Garnish your wages directly from your employer.
- Seize and sell property in serious cases.
- Refer the account to an outside collection agency, which adds collection costs to the balance.
- Continue adding penalties and interest until the balance is paid.

The Department also shares information with the IRS. A federal audit adjustment usually produces a Colorado bill, and vice versa. If you have a problem with one agency, assume the other one will find out.

## Option 1: A Colorado payment plan

For most people, a payment plan (the Department calls it a payment agreement) is the primary tool. In broad terms the Department offers two tiers:

- **A short-term plan** that can be set up with little or no financial disclosure, generally through the Department's Revenue Online portal, as long as the full balance is paid within the allowed term.
- **A longer-term plan** for taxpayers who cannot pay within the short-term window. This requires a completed Statement of Income and Expenses and supporting documents, and the Department sets the monthly payment based on what it concludes you can afford.

Two features surprise taxpayers coming from the IRS side. First, Colorado has no equivalent of the IRS's "currently not collectible" hardship status. If you cannot pay, the Department still expects a plan. Second, Colorado plans are shorter than the IRS's 72-month streamlined agreements and the Department is less flexible about missed payments. A default usually means the full balance is due immediately and enforced collection resumes.

Our earlier article on the [Department's payment options for individual income taxes](/articles/colorado-department-of-revenue-payment-options-for-individual-income-taxes/) covers the mechanics in more detail.

## Option 2: A Colorado offer in compromise

An offer in compromise settles a tax debt for less than the full amount owed. Colorado does have an offer program, but it is narrower than the IRS program, and the eligibility rules catch many people off guard.

As a general rule, the Department will consider an offer only after the IRS has accepted an offer in compromise covering the same tax years. Colorado then evaluates whether to accept a proportionate settlement of the state liability. In practice that means the path to a Colorado offer usually runs through a federal offer first, and a taxpayer who owes only Colorado, or whose IRS offer was rejected, has very limited settlement options at the state level.

Because of this, "settle your Colorado tax debt for pennies on the dollar" pitches deserve skepticism. Our attorneys have written before about [tax resolution companies](/articles/beware-of-tax-resolution-companies/) that sell offers to taxpayers who do not qualify for them.

## Option 3: Penalty waivers

Colorado penalties on late-filed returns and late payments add up quickly, and they compound with interest. The Department may waive penalties when the taxpayer shows good cause, such as a serious illness, a natural disaster, reliance on incorrect written advice, or another circumstance outside the taxpayer's control. Interest is treated differently and is generally not waived.

A penalty waiver request should be specific, documented, and filed before the balance is referred to outside collections. A one-line "please waive my penalties" rarely works. A well-supported request often removes a meaningful share of the balance and makes the remaining payment plan affordable.

## Option 4: Fixing the bill itself

Many Colorado balances are wrong. The most common causes we see:

- **Unfiled returns.** When you do not file, the Department may estimate your tax from IRS data and third-party records. Those estimates ignore deductions, credits, and withholding. Filing the actual return often cuts the balance dramatically.
- **IRS adjustments that were later reversed.** If the IRS changed your federal return and Colorado followed, but you then won an appeal or audit reconsideration with the IRS, Colorado needs to be told. It will not fix the state bill on its own.
- **Residency and part-year issues.** People who moved into or out of Colorado are frequently taxed on income that belongs to another state.
- **Misapplied payments.** Payments credited to the wrong tax year or the wrong spouse's account create phantom balances.

Before agreeing to pay anything, confirm that the assessed amount is actually correct. Disputing an incorrect assessment is a matter of Colorado tax procedure with strict deadlines. Once a protest window closes, the balance becomes final even if it is wrong.

## Option 5: Bankruptcy, in limited cases

Some older income tax debts can be discharged in bankruptcy, at both the federal and state level, if the returns were filed and enough time has passed under the rules for tax claims. Bankruptcy is rarely the right first move for a tax problem, and recently assessed or unfiled-return liabilities do not qualify. It belongs in the conversation only after the other options are evaluated, and only with counsel who understands both the tax and bankruptcy rules.

## Mistakes that make a Colorado tax debt worse

1. **Ignoring the notices.** The Department's timeline from first notice to garnishment is shorter than most taxpayers expect.
2. **Setting up a plan you cannot afford.** A defaulted agreement is worse than no agreement.
3. **Paying the state and ignoring the IRS, or the reverse.** A resolution that handles only one agency usually unravels.
4. **Hiring a national "tax relief" company.** These firms rarely understand Colorado procedure, and they often promise offers that Colorado will not accept.
5. **Waiting to file.** Unfiled returns block every resolution option and trigger inflated estimated assessments.

## When to involve a Colorado tax attorney

Not every Colorado balance requires a lawyer. If the amount is modest, the return is correct, and you can pay within the short-term plan period, Revenue Online may be all you need.

Talk to a tax attorney when the balance is large relative to your income, when you have unfiled years, when you owe both the IRS and Colorado, when the Department has already filed a lien or issued a garnishment, or when you believe the assessed amount is wrong. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can I settle my Colorado state tax debt for less than I owe?

Only through an offer in compromise, and Colorado generally considers one only after the IRS has accepted an offer for the same tax years. Most Colorado balances are resolved through a payment plan, penalty waiver, or correction of the assessment rather than a reduced settlement.

### How long does a Colorado Department of Revenue payment plan last?

Colorado offers a shorter plan with little financial disclosure and a longer plan that requires a full Statement of Income and Expenses. Both are shorter than typical IRS agreements, and a missed payment can default the plan.

### Does Colorado have a hardship or "currently not collectible" status?

No. Unlike the IRS, the Department does not suspend collection because a taxpayer cannot afford to pay. It expects a payment agreement or full payment.

### Will the Colorado Department of Revenue garnish my wages?

Yes. Wage garnishment and bank levies are standard Colorado collection tools once notices go unanswered, and they can be issued without a court order.

### Should I resolve my IRS debt or my Colorado debt first?

Usually both at once, with the federal side leading, because Colorado's offer program depends on the IRS outcome and because the financial disclosures you give one agency should match what you give the other. A tax attorney can sequence the two so that one resolution does not undermine the other.
